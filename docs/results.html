1 · The headline

Same workload, same code path, both architectures. Native transfer, medians over 487 and 323 successful transactions.

LevelzkSync Erakind OP Sepoliakindratio

Trustless (t₃)35.82 minobserved 7.01 daysderivedOP 281× slower

Which rollup is "faster" depends entirely on which trust assumption was meant. At full trust the two are within ten seconds of each other. The optimistic rollup then pulls sharply ahead at partial trust, because it posts batches to Ethereum every few minutes where zkSync posts roughly every half hour. And then it loses by more than two orders of magnitude at trustless finality.

That crossover is what a single-number latency benchmark cannot show, and it is the result this project exists to produce.

The kind column is not decoration

zkSync's t₃ is observed: a proof was verified in an Ethereum block whose timestamp we read, and the transaction hash is in every row. OP's is derived: nothing happens when a challenge window closes, so it is a deadline computed from the output proposal plus the challenge period read from the OptimismPortal. The two must never be tabulated as though they were the same kind of fact.

OP's cost is reported unavailable rather than estimated. An OP Stack chain does not expose how many transactions share a batch without decoding the blob, so there is no denominator. A guess would have been worse than a gap.

L2 gas is not comparable across the two. zkSync's gas model folds pubdata into the gas figure and OP's does not, so 83,513 against 21,000 is not a fourfold difference in work. Only cost in ETH is comparable, and even that needs the L1 gas price recorded beside it — which every row carries.

5 · How much results vary between days

Eleven comparisons across cells run on more than one day. Median divergence 13.3%, worst 65.7%. The split matters more than the headline:

LevelDay-to-day variation
Full trust (t₁)0.1% to 0.9% on small batches — highly reproducible
Partial trust (t₂)20% to 66%
Trustless (t₃)1% to 54%

This is exactly the shape to expect. t₁ measures a sequencer accepting a transaction, which is a fast and fairly deterministic operation. t₂ and t₃ depend on where in a batch interval the submission happened to fall, which is close to arbitrary. Settlement medians are the ones to treat with caution, and this is the number that says how much.

Two consequences for the report.

Mainnet proves about 3.4× slower than the testnet — 38.38 minutes against 11.2 — so every testnet trustless figure understates production by a factor that can now be stated rather than gestured at.

OP Mainnet's proof maturity delay is 7.00 days, identical to OP Sepolia's. Testnets are widely assumed to shorten their challenge windows; this one does not. That confirms against production what the testnet reading suggested, and it is the strongest support for treating every optimistic t₃ as derived.

8 · Threats to validity

Every limitation known at the time of writing. Several were discovered by measurement rather than anticipated.

Measurement

  • t₁ is an observation, not a chain fact. It is when a receipt became visible to us, which exceeds the L2 block's own timestamp by a median of 17.95 s on batched submissions. It is an upper bound on full-trust latency. Both values are stored so the gap is visible.
  • t₁ can never be finer than the polling interval, capped at 2 s here. A single transaction resolving in 0.6 s is measured at that resolution.
  • L1 block timestamps have second granularity and are set by the proposer. Negligible against intervals of minutes, but real.
  • Public RPC endpoints introduce variable latency outside our control, and one of them was found serving state 38 days stale.
  • Submission came from one machine in one geographic region, with one account.

The optimistic side is weaker than the ZK side

  • t₂ on OP is estimated, not observed. An OP Stack chain exposes no mapping from a transaction to the batch carrying it, so the batch is matched by scanning the L1 forward from the L2 block's origin. 16 of 519 OP rows (3%) have a t₂ within five seconds of submission, which is implausibly fast given batches are posted every few minutes — those are almost certainly matched to a batch that does not contain the transaction. The median is not materially affected, but the minimum is meaningless and the field should be read as an approximation with a few percent error.
  • t₃ on OP is derived, not observed. It is a deadline computed from a proposal timestamp plus a challenge period. No event occurs at that moment and no transaction hash can be produced for it.
  • L1 cost on OP is unavailable, for the same reason as t₂.

Scope

  • Peak throughput was not measured. Saturating a shared public sequencer would degrade a service other people depend on and would measure our own rate limits. Achieved throughput at a stated submission rate is reported instead — and at 0.003 to 0.008 tx/s it reflects our submission pattern, not any capacity of the networks.
  • Proving time and cost are not measured, only the L1 gas of verifying a proof. No GPU was available.
  • Testnet is not mainnet. Proof cadence differs by 3.4×. Costs are testnet L1 gas priced at a mainnet ETH rate, which is an illustration rather than a measurement of what anyone paid.
  • Settlement repetitions are still accumulating. F2 is at 29 of 60 repetitions. Percentiles on t₂ and t₃ will tighten.
  • Blob DA cost is a Sepolia artefact, as noted in §3.

Things that cannot be measured this way at all

  • Per-transaction data-availability size. A blob costs 131,072 bytes whether filled or not, and a batch carries every user's transactions. DA per transaction is a batch average identical across workloads. The workload difference is real but appears in L2 gas: 83,513 against 122,434.
  • Cost as a function of our own batch size. Seven runs of sizes 1 to 50 all landed in one batch of 875 and cost exactly the same per transaction. The denominator is the rollup's batch count, which we do not control.

9 · How to check any number here

Every row carries the hashes that produced it, so no figure has to be taken on trust.

  1. Open bench/export/raw_transactions.csv — one row per transaction, all four absolute timestamps, both L1 hashes.
  2. Pick any row. Open its hash in the L2 explorer and its l1_commit_tx and l1_prove_tx in Etherscan.
  3. The block timestamps on those two Ethereum transactions are t₂ and t₃.
  4. bench/export/assumptions.txt states the ETH rate, where it came from and when, plus every declared limitation.
  5. Each figure has a .csv beside it containing the numbers behind it.
python -m bench.check_data     # asserts the rows describe something possible
python -m bench.export         # regenerate every table
python -m bench.analysis.figures

Latencies and prices are computed at export time from absolute timestamps and raw wei. Nothing in the stored rows depends on an assumption, which is why correcting the ETH rate — it had been wrong by a factor of two — cost one command rather than a repeat of the experiment.
